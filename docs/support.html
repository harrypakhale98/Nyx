Common questions

Where did Calendar and Learn go?

Since version 1.2, Nyx has four tabs: Tonight, Parks, Plan and Journal. The month for one park and the trip planner are in Plan. The essays are in Settings, under About the sky.

Hollow nights, "No cloud forecast yet" and "Early look"

A full cloud forecast counts for about three days ahead. Further out, a half-filled night is an early look: the forecast is eased toward the park's usual clouds for that month. A hollow night has no forecast yet and uses the usual clouds alone, so its score can change when a forecast arrives. Pull down to refresh when you have a connection, and check that Cloud forecasts is on under Settings → Your privacy.

"Clouds limit tonight to 64", or a score lower than its parts

The four parts add up, then the weakest one can cap the total: heavy cloud, a bright sky glow, smoke or short hours of true darkness. The line under the score names the cap that applies.

"Access not checked" or "Park alerts are busy"

Nyx could not get current park alerts. Use the link to the park's current conditions on nps.gov, or contact the park before traveling.

No nearby parks

Widen the radius or choose a different starting point. The radius is a straight line, not driving distance.

Where do you start from?

Tonight asks where you start from. Choose Near me to use your location while you use Nyx, or choose a starting point: a US city or a park. Location is optional, and your choice stays on your device.

Where to stay

On a park's page, The place lists the park's campgrounds from the National Park Service. Reservation links open only Recreation.gov or nps.gov. The list is asked for all 63 parks at once, at most once a week, and not in Low Data Mode.

From your starting point tonight

At the foot of Tonight, Nyx works out the Moon, true darkness, planets, meteor showers and eclipses for your starting point, on your device, with no request. It has no light map for homes, so it does not score the sky there.

Follow this night

On a park's night, Follow this night puts the night on your Lock Screen as a Live Activity. It starts on its own about 30 minutes before sunset, and no server is involved.

No reminders

Save a park and turn on reminders in Settings. Only nights scoring 90 or more, up to five days ahead and with a recent, full cloud forecast, qualify, and at most one a week for each park. Reminders are recalculated whenever Nyx opens and, when iOS allows, about every six hours in the background.

"No true darkness tonight"

In Alaska's summer the Sun never sinks 18° below the horizon, so astronomical darkness never arrives. Nyx caps those nights below 40.

Night vision

Turn it on with the flashlight button at the top left of every tab, in Settings, or from Control Center. Lower your screen brightness too; outside field mode, Nyx does not change it for you.

Your journal

Entries and photos stay on your iPhone or iPad. Back up your device with Apple's settings; Nyx has no cloud sync or account recovery. To move a journal to another device, use Export in the Journal tab and open the file on the other device.

Translate

When Nyx runs in a language other than English, a Translate button under park alerts and ranger programs opens Apple's translation, which runs on your device.

Assistive Access

When your iPhone or iPad uses Assistive Access, Nyx shows one answer in large type: tonight's darkest park within reach, and your saved parks, each with one word for the night, the Moon, when true darkness begins and any closure.

An age range request

In a few US states, the law asks apps to check the age range of new accounts. Where iOS says this applies to your account, it may show its own age range sheet once. Nyx keeps nothing from it and nothing in Nyx depends on the answer.

Search at the largest text sizes

With the largest accessibility text sizes, Parks shows its search field in the page, under its title, instead of in the navigation bar.

Apple Watch

"No recent forecast" on the watch

The watch makes no network requests. Its cloud forecasts come from Nyx on your paired iPhone: open Nyx on the iPhone with a connection, and the watch updates. Until then, the watch scores each night with the park's usual clouds and says so.

"Models 72–89" on the watch

When the three forecast models disagree about a night's clouds, the watch shows their range beside the score, as the dial on your iPhone does. The range comes from Nyx on your iPhone with the forecasts; the watch shows it only on nights it scores from a full forecast.

Red at dusk

With the Automatic palette, the watch turns red from dusk to dawn at the park on Tonight. Choose another palette in Nyx on the watch, or use the Red light control.

Saved parks

The watch shows the parks you saved on your iPhone.

Apple Vision Pro

Opening the sky

Choose a park in the window, then "Stand under this sky". "Leave the sky" closes it. The clock at the bottom of the window steps nights and moves through the hours, and the sky follows.

"Usual clouds"

Nyx on Vision Pro uses the cloud forecast when it has one and each park's usual clouds for the month otherwise (or when you switch forecasts off in Your privacy). For park alerts and field mode, use Nyx on iPhone or iPad.

Which way is south?

The sky is computed for the park and night you choose, with south ahead of you, where the Milky Way, Moon and planets cross. It is not turned to your room's real directions; a plaque at your feet says so. When Nyx has a forecast for that night, clouds follow it, and their shapes are illustrative.

Names in the sky

Tap a planet, a bright star, the Moon or the Milky Way core to see its name, its height and its direction. Constellations in the window toolbar shows or hides the figures. Drag across the sky to turn the night by hand.

The Moon on your table

Open it from the window toolbar. It is lit at that night's phase; drag to turn it, and it turns back to the face we always see.

About the data

Sun and Moon positions are calculated on your device; moonrise and moonset agree with the U.S. Naval Observatory to within a few minutes, and terrain can shift what you see. Light-pollution classes are conservative estimates, not measurements. Heavy smoke or haze from the Copernicus (CAMS) forecast can cap the score; telescope seeing is not forecast. The Milky Way core, planets (to about a degree) and meteor showers are also calculated on your device; shower rates are a rough guide from the International Meteor Organization calendar, and lunar eclipse times are Eclipse Predictions by Fred Espenak, NASA's GSFC.
