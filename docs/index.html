A dark-sky planner for the 63 US national parks

Where, and which night, for the darkest sky.

Nyx weighs the Moon, the clouds, the sky glow and the hours of true darkness for every park and every night, and turns them into one score from 0 to 100. It shows its reasons, puts park closures beside the number, and says what it cannot know.

The Darkness Score

One number, with its reasons.

Four things decide whether you will see the Milky Way. Nyx adds them up for each park and night, then lets the weakest one hold the total down, because one problem is enough to spoil a night.

93 Pristine DARKNESS / 100
  • Moon39 / 40

    Its phase, and how high it stands through the hours of true darkness.

  • Clouds25 / 25

    Averaged over the dark hours only. For the coming week, three weather models are compared.

  • Sky glow15 / 20

    The park's estimated Bortle class, with satellite night lights to show where the glow comes from.

  • True darkness14 / 15

    Hours with the Sun 18° below the horizon. In an Alaskan summer there are none, and Nyx says so.

The weakest part can cap the rest. Heavy cloud, a bright sky or wildfire smoke holds the score down, and the app names the cap under the number. An overcast night never reads as good.

Score bands:

  1. Poorbelow 40
  2. Fair40 and up
  3. Good60 and up
  4. Excellent75 and up
  5. Pristine90 and up

Tonight

The darkest sky within reach.

Open Nyx and it shows the best-scoring parks around you, or around a US city or a park you choose, with the reasons on the same screen.

  • A radius you set, measured honestly: "within 200 mi as the crow flies".
  • National Park Service alerts beside the score. A dark sky is no use behind a closed road.
  • Location is optional and stays on your device.

Calendar and time river

Choose your night.

A month at a glance, each night a small sky sized by its score, with the best run of nights near the new Moon marked. Scrub thirty nights ahead and watch the Moon change shape as you go.

A forecast is reliable for a few days. After that Nyx leans on each park's usual clouds, so the calendar draws the difference:

  • Full forecast
  • Early look
  • No forecast yet

What's up tonight

The Milky Way, and when to look.

The galactic core timed for the park you chose, the planets with where and when they rise, meteor showers with an honest rate range, and lunar eclipses the park can actually see.

  • Computed on your iPhone. Planets are placed to about a degree through 2032.
  • Times are park-local, wherever you plan from.

Field mode

At the park, the screen turns red.

Your eyes need twenty to thirty minutes to adapt to the dark, and one glance at a bright screen undoes much of it. Field mode dims everything to deep red, counts down to true darkness and turns the real sky to wherever you point the phone, with no camera.

  • A dark-adaptation clock, alarms for the core and moonset, and a Live Activity on the Lock Screen.
  • Red text on black measures 6.2 : 1, readable without breaking your night vision.

Before and after

Plan the trip. Keep the night.

Tell Nyx which nights you are free and it suggests a park for each, with a route drawn as stars. Afterwards, a private journal turns every night out into a star in your own constellation.

  • Add any suggested night to your calendar.
  • Notes and photos stay on your device. Export the journal as a file you keep.

For more people

Hear the night. Feel the Moon.

Every custom control in Nyx speaks a summary of its data. "Listen to tonight" plays the shape of the night as tones, with a transcript, and audio graphs let VoiceOver users hear a month of darkness.

  • Designed for VoiceOver and the largest text sizes, with a red mode for night-adapted eyes.
  • Haptics can follow the Moon's phase; the time river ticks once per night.
  • Step-free notes for viewing spots, quoted from the park's own accessibility page where it says something, and marked unknown where it does not.

iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch, Apple Vision Pro

The same sky, wherever you look.

iPad

The planner, the score and its reasons side by side in wide windows, in any orientation, with the iPhone layout in narrow ones.

Apple Watch

Tonight's score on your wrist, the Digital Crown to look ahead, a dark-adaptation clock that taps when your eyes are ready, and complications that turn red at dusk.

Honest by design

It says what it cannot know.

A planning app is only useful if you can trust it, so Nyx draws its uncertainty instead of hiding it.

Models side by side

For the next week, three weather models are compared. When they disagree, Nyx draws the range and says "Models differ".

Usual clouds, labelled

Beyond the forecast, each park's usual clouds for the month stand in, from ten years of climate records, and those nights look different.

Estimates called estimates

Bortle classes, sky glow and meteor rates carry the word. Moonrise agrees with the U.S. Naval Observatory to within minutes.

Access first

A high score never means a road is open. Closures sit beside the number. If alerts can't load, Nyx says so and links to the park's current conditions.

Privacy

Nothing about you leaves your device.

No account, no ads, no analytics, no tracking. The astronomy runs on your device, so the Moon, twilight and the Milky Way work offline. Your journal never leaves your device.

This website is the same: no cookies, no scripts, no analytics.

Data Not Collected

Nyx asks three public services for data, and each has its own switch in Settings:

  • api.open-meteo.comCloud forecasts for all 63 parks, asked for by the parks' public coordinates, never yours
  • air-quality-api.open-meteo.comSmoke and haze forecasts for the same parks
  • developer.nps.govPark alerts and campgrounds for all 63 parks, and ranger night-sky programs for the park you open
