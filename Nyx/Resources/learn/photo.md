Your first Milky Way photo

You do not need expensive gear to photograph the Milky Way. You need a dark sky, a steady camera and a few settings you choose yourself, because a camera's automatic modes are made for daylight.

Start with a tripod. Every setting below keeps the shutter open for many seconds, and a light breeze or the press of a button will blur the stars. Use the camera's two-second timer or a remote, so your hand is off the camera when the exposure begins.

Use the widest, fastest lens you have. A focal length of about 14 to 24 mm on a full-frame camera takes in a long stretch of the band. Open the aperture all the way, or close to it: f/2.8 is a good target, and f/2 or f/1.8 is better if your lens allows it.

Choose the shutter time with care. The Earth turns, so the stars drift across the frame, and a long exposure stretches points into short streaks. A rough guide called the 500 rule divides 500 by your focal length: at 20 mm, that is about 25 seconds. On cameras with smaller sensors, or for pin-sharp stars, go shorter. Somewhere between 15 and 25 seconds is a good start.

Set the ISO between 1600 and 6400. Try 3200 first, look at the result and adjust. A brighter picture with some grain is easier to fix later than a dark one.

Focus by hand. Autofocus cannot find faint stars. Switch to manual focus, point at the brightest star or planet in the sky, magnify the live view as far as it goes, and turn the ring until that point is as small as you can make it. Then leave the ring alone; a strip of tape helps.

Shoot in RAW. It keeps far more of the faint detail and lets you set the color afterward. A white balance near 4000 K is a good place to begin.

Time it. The Milky Way's bright center is the part most people want, and it is up for only part of the year and part of the night. Nyx's What's up shows when the core is up during true darkness at each park, where it will stand and whether the Moon will be down. The calendar's ringed nights mark the darkest stretches.

Give the picture a place. A tree, a rock formation or a ridgeline against the sky turns a picture of stars into a picture of one night somewhere. Arrive in daylight to find your spot and frame it. Light painting and stray lights can spoil other people's photos and night vision, so keep lights off when others are near, and follow the park's rules. Flying a drone is not allowed in national parks.

Phones can do this too. On a tripod, a recent iPhone's Night mode can hold its shutter open for up to 30 seconds.
