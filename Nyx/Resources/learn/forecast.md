What a forecast can't tell you

A cloud forecast is a computer model's best guess at the sky, worked out on a grid whose boxes are a few miles, or several kilometers, across. It is often right, and it is never certain. Knowing where it is weakest helps you read a score honestly.

Models disagree. Nyx compares three of them, from the United States, Europe and Germany. When all three show a clear night, the forecast is on firm ground. When one shows clear skies and another shows cloud, the truth could be either, and the park's page says the models disagree and gives the range of scores they would lead to. A disagreement is not a bad forecast. It is the forecast telling you how sure it is.

High, thin cloud is hard to judge. A veil of ice crystals miles up can dim faint stars and spread moonlight across the sky while the forecast still looks mostly clear. Or it can be counted as cloud while bright stars shine straight through it. A percentage says how much of the sky is covered, not how thick the cover is.

Smoke and haze are not clouds. Wildfire smoke can travel a thousand miles (1,600 km) and dull the Milky Way on a cloudless night. Nyx reads a separate smoke and haze forecast and caps the score when it is heavy, but smoke forecasts are rougher than cloud forecasts, and dust or damp air near the ground can wash out the horizon without showing up in either.

Far nights fade toward the usual clouds. A forecast for tomorrow is far better than a guess. A forecast for ten days from now is barely better than knowing what the weather here usually does in that month. So Nyx uses a cloud forecast in full for about three days, then eases it toward the park's usual clouds, and beyond ten days uses the usual clouds alone. Those nights are drawn half-filled or hollow, because their scores will change as the night comes closer. Check again a day or two before you go.

Local weather hides between the grid points. Fog can fill a valley or roll in from the coast while a ridge a few hundred feet (about 100 m) higher stays clear. On still nights, cold air pools in low places. As the air cools toward its dew point, dew forms on lenses, eyepieces and windshields; a hand warmer against the lens helps. Mountains often build their own clouds in the afternoon, and these usually fade after sunset, though not always.

What helps most is watching the sky yourself. Look west in the late afternoon, where most weather comes from. Ask a ranger about local fog. And when the forecast is unsure, choose a park with more than one good night within reach, and let the sky decide.
