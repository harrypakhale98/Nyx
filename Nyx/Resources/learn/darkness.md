A sky worth protecting

A dark sky is more than a place to see stars. It is a habitat. Long before electric light, plants and animals lived with a steady rhythm of bright days and dark nights, and they still do.

Light scattered in the atmosphere creates sky glow. The faint band of the Milky Way is the first thing it hides; the brightest stars remain. A world atlas of night sky brightness, published in 2016, put the scale plainly: “The Milky Way is hidden from more than one-third of humanity, including 60% of Europeans and nearly 80% of North Americans.” A national park can be the first place someone sees what is missing.

The change is also fast. From 2011 to 2022, people around the world counted the stars they could see and reported them to Globe at Night, a citizen-science project. A study of 51,351 of those counts, published in 2023, found that “The number of visible stars decreased by an amount that can be explained by an increase in sky brightness of 7 to 10% per year in the human visible band.” That is faster than satellites show, partly because the color of outdoor light has changed.

Each park's page in Nyx names the towns whose light rises on its horizon, from NASA's satellite night lights. From Death Valley, the brightest glow lies east, toward Las Vegas. The same page draws the park's sky twice, at its estimated class and at a city's, so the difference can be seen rather than imagined.

Darkness does not mean using no light. It means light where it helps, when it is needed, no brighter than the task. A shielded light aimed at the ground serves a path better than one shining into the sky.

Park staff protect night skies by improving outdoor lighting and measuring sky brightness, and International Dark Sky certification recognizes that work. It is not a promise that every corner is equally dark. That is why Nyx calls each park's Bortle class an estimate: a visitor center has more light than a remote overlook.

At the park, a modest red light is enough for moving around. Headlights can go off once you are safely parked, and a beam kept low stays out of other observers' eyes. A ranger can tell you where you may stay after dark.

If you would like to add to the record, count the stars you can see on a clear night and report them to Globe at Night. It takes a few minutes, from a park or from your own street, and each count shows a little more of how the night is changing.
