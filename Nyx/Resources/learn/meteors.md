Watching a meteor shower

A meteor is a grain of dust, often smaller than a grain of sand, striking the air at tens of kilometers a second and burning about a hundred kilometers up. A shower happens when Earth passes through the trail of dust a comet or asteroid has left along its orbit. The same trails return on the same dates every year, which is why the Perseids come in August and the Geminids in December.

Every meteor in a shower seems to fly out of one small patch of sky, the radiant, which gives the shower its name: the Geminids from Gemini, the Perseids from Perseus. You do not need to look at the radiant. Meteors cross the whole sky, and the longest, most graceful ones appear well away from it. Face whichever part of the sky is darkest and clearest, about halfway up.

The rate you read in calendars is the zenithal hourly rate, or ZHR: the number one observer would see in an hour under a perfect sky, with the radiant straight overhead. You will almost always see fewer. A radiant low in the sky hides most of the shower below the horizon. Moonlight and light pollution drown the faint meteors, which are most of them. Nyx works out a rough rate for each park and night from the radiant's height, the Moon and that park's estimated sky brightness. Treat it as a guide, not a count. Showers vary from year to year, and a few can surprise.

The Moon matters more than anything you control. A bright Moon can cut the count in half or worse. The best hours are usually after midnight, when your side of Earth faces forward into the dust, and especially when the Moon has set.

Lie down. A reclining chair or a sleeping pad lets you take in the most sky with the least strain. Dress for a temperature well below what the forecast says; lying still on the ground is colder than standing. Keep your phone dim and red, or away. Your eyes need twenty to thirty minutes to adapt to the dark, and one bright screen undoes it.

Then be patient. Meteors come in clusters and lulls. Ten quiet minutes can be followed by three in a row. Give it at least an hour. Many people who say they saw nothing gave up early.

No telescope or binoculars are needed. Meteors are too quick and too spread out. The instrument is your own eyes, adapted to the dark, under the widest sky you can find.
