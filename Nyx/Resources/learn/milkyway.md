Finding the Milky Way

The Milky Way is our own galaxy seen from the inside: a band of light from countless distant stars, crossed by dark lanes of dust. Its brightest part, the galactic center, lies toward the constellations Sagittarius and Scorpius. Seeing it well depends on four things Nyx already weighs: a dark site, a dark Moon, clear air and enough true darkness.

Season matters most, and so does where you stand on Earth. The bright center lies far to the south in the sky, near the Sun's path in December, so in December and January it is lost in daylight everywhere. North of the equator it rises before dawn from about March, stands highest in the southern sky on June, July and August nights, and sinks into the southwest soon after dusk by October. It never climbs far there: about 20 to 30° up from the American Southwest, southern Spain or Japan, barely 10° from Germany or southern England, and from most of Alaska and northern Scandinavia it never clears the horizon.

South of the equator the same center rides high. From southern Africa, Australia or Chile it passes nearly overhead on June, July and August nights, the southern winter, and it is in the night sky from about March to October. In American Samoa it climbs to about 75°. In the other months the fainter band still arches across the sky, past Cassiopeia and Orion in the north and past Orion and the Southern Cross in the south, and it rewards a dark night too.

The Moon decides which nights are worth the drive. A bright Moon washes out the faint glow, even at the darkest park. Look for nights near the new moon, or nights when the Moon sets early or rises late and leaves hours of true darkness. The ringed nights in Plan and the lighter, moonlit stretches of the sky arc show both.

Give your eyes time. Full dark adaptation takes twenty to thirty minutes, and one glance at a bright screen undoes much of it. Turn on night-vision mode, lower your screen brightness, and use a dim red light on the trail.

North of the equator, look low and to the south, and choose a place with an open southern horizon: a desert flat, a ridgeline overlook, a lake shore. Cliffs, trees and canyon walls can hide the brightest part even under a perfect sky. South of the equator, look up: in winter the center stands overhead, where little can hide it. Haze and wildfire smoke dim it as surely as clouds do, which is one reason no forecast can promise what you will see.

Photographs show more color and detail than the eye can. To the eye, the Milky Way is a soft, silvery river with darker gaps. That is not a disappointment. It is the real thing: light that has traveled for thousands of years to reach you.
