Reading the Bortle scale

The Bortle scale describes how a night sky looks to an observer. It runs from class 1, an exceptionally dark sky, to class 9, a bright inner-city sky. Lower numbers mean less artificial light. The scale is useful language for comparing places, but it is not a precise instrument reading and it cannot predict the weather.

Under a dark sky, faint stars become easier to see and the Milky Way may show structure instead of appearing as a barely visible band. Near a city, a bright background can wash out those details. You might still enjoy the Moon, planets and bright constellations. A high Bortle number does not mean there is nothing to see; it means the faintest parts of the sky have more competition.

Classes 1 and 2 describe very dark skies. Class 3 is a rural sky where artificial light may be visible near the horizon. Class 4 is a transition toward suburban conditions. Classes 5 and 6 bring more sky glow, and classes 7 through 9 describe increasingly bright skies. These are broad descriptions, not a checklist that every observer will agree on perfectly.

Your experience depends on more than artificial light. A full moon can brighten an otherwise remote park. Haze and smoke scatter light. A cloud can reflect light from a town or hide the stars altogether. Trees, cliffs and mountains can block your view without changing the darkness of the open sky. Even your eyes need time to adjust after a bright screen or a car's headlights.

Nyx includes a conservative Bortle estimate for each park. It is a planning assumption, not a measurement made at the moment you arrive. A large park may contain very different viewing conditions. An estimate for the park cannot describe every campground, overlook or trail. This is why the app labels the class as an estimate and keeps weather, moonlight and the length of darkness separate in the score breakdown.

When you record a journal entry, choose the class that seems closest to the sky you observed. There is no need to turn your evening into an examination. Note whether the Milky Way was visible, where a bright horizon appeared, and whether haze or the Moon changed the view. Those notes may be more useful on your next trip than the number alone.

Try comparing the same place on different nights. A moonless evening after clear weather can feel very different from a humid night under a bright Moon. That difference is part of learning to read the sky. Use the scale to describe an experience, use the forecast to prepare, and give yourself time to look before deciding what you can see.
