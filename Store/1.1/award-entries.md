## Shared framing (use as the spine for every entry)

**Problem.** People drive hours into national parks to see the Milky Way, then arrive under a bright Moon, a cloud deck or a city's glow. The question that decides the trip, where should I go and on which night for the darkest sky, has no good answer. Weather apps show clouds, almanacs show the Moon, and nobody joins them for a park.

**Solution.** Nyx gives each of the 63 US national parks a nightly Darkness Score from 0 to 100: 40% Moon (illumination and how long the Moon is below the horizon), 25% cloud cover, 20% estimated sky glow, 15% length of true darkness, explained in four meters. It says what it does not know (forecast horizon, three-model agreement, smoke as a caveat, estimates named as estimates), runs its astronomy on the device so it works offline, and is built for night-adapted eyes: red night vision, and in 1.1 a field mode. It has no account and collects no data.

**Impact.** There are no usage numbers yet. Do not invent any. Evidence that exists today and may be stated:
- The App Privacy label reads Data Not Collected. Three optional network services, each switchable; no analytics or tracking SDKs; no third-party code.
- Apple's automated accessibility audit passes on every screen in both palettes on iOS 26 and 27; measured contrast ratios are published (`Research/contrast.json`); the physical-device review is open and said to be open (`AUDIT.md`).
- Astronomy checked against USNO and PyEphem references (`Research/accuracy.md`): solar rise and set within 0.5 minutes, lunar within 3.7 minutes on the samples tested.
- Free, with no purchase or subscription.
After launch, replace "so far" lines with measured facts: App Store downloads, ratings, accessibility-community feedback, parks or groups that link to it.
